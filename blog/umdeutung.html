Umdeutung of Umdeutung

Dec 22, 2025
1. Heisenberg's 1925 "Umdeutung" (reinterpretation) paper reinterpreted classical mechanics in quantum terms. This essay is itself a reinterpretation, an umdeutung of the umdeutung, examining how different mathematical representations of the same physics shape understanding and pedagogy.

In June 1925, Werner Heisenberg published his "Umdeutung" paper, the "reinterpretation" that launched what he called "matrix mechanics" and what we now call quantum mechanics because of Paul Dirac, who always credited Heisenberg as having "discovered" quantum mechanics in his lectures. And I think, in that Umdeutung paper, there was a simple equation that could have been read two ways in retrospect. We study quantum mechanics with one and little with the other. This is about the other1.

What follows was understood by the early 1930s and is familiar to experts. But I think that a subtle choice in how we read this equation had long-lasting consequences for how we teach, think about, and develop quantum theory. And that the alternative path, always available and used but not quite emphasized, deserves another look.

1. Where It All Began

In the early 1920s, before quantum mechanics existed as a theory, spectroscopists had accumulated precise data on the light emitted by atoms. Hydrogen atoms, when excited, emitted photons at specific frequencies i.e. discrete spectral lines that formed predictable patterns. Among these patterns, one stood out for its mathematical simplicity, which was the Ritz combination principle.

If you measure the frequency of light emitted when an electron transitions from energy level 6 to level 3, and separately from level 3 to level 1, these frequencies obey:

ω63 + ω31 = ω61

2. The Ritz principle was purely empirical in the 1920s, which means, no one knew why frequencies added this way. Later, Heisenberg's genius insight made this composition law the foundation of the new quantum theory, rather than trying to derive it from classical mechanics.

The frequency for the direct transition from 6 to 1 equals the sum of the two intermediate frequencies. It's as if the photon could "go through" the intermediate state at level 3, with the frequencies simply adding2.

Werner Heisenberg, working on the island of Heligoland in June 1925, proposed an answer. Quantum theory should not be built on unobservable classical concepts like electron orbits or continuous trajectories and instead, it should be constructed entirely from observable quantities like the frequencies and intensities of spectral lines. The mathematical structure encoding how these observables combine would be the new matrix mechanics.

Heisenberg's key insight was a composition law for quantum transition amplitudes. If A(n,m) represents the amplitude for a transition from state m to state n, then to find the amplitude B(n,m) for a different observable (or the square of the amplitude), you sum over all possible intermediate states j:

B(n,n-m) = Σj=-∞∞ A(n,n-j) A(n-j,n-m)

This equation was the birth of quantum mechanics. The frequencies obeyed Ritz's rule by construction i.e. they added when transitions composed. But this seemingly simple sum would shape how we think about quantum mechanics until Richard Feynman.

The question I want explore is: What if this equation had been interpreted differently from the start?

2. Algebra vs. Calculus

When Max Born and Pascual Jordan saw Heisenberg's composition law in September 1925, just three months after the Umdeutung paper, they immediately recognized that this was matrix multiplication. If you arrange the transition amplitudes A into an array with rows and columns indexed by the quantum numbers n and m, then Heisenberg's sum is exactly what matrices do when multiplied. And hence Matrix mechanics was born. Born and Jordan formalized it, Heisenberg developed it further, and by early 1926 the mathematical structure was clear that quantum states are vectors in a Hilbert space, observables are Hermitian operators (infinite-dimensional matrices), and evolution proceeds via unitary transformations. This became the standard formulation, the one taught in every textbook, the language of quantum information theory and quantum computing and one which even I learnt over the past 3 years when studying all these.

But, I think, that sum could be read another way.

3. Heisenberg's discrete indices could represent sampled points from a continuum. The sum becomes a Riemann sum approximating an integral. Why? Because that's how you move from discrete quantum numbers to continuous position variables.

Consider the indices n, m, j not as discrete labels for energy levels, but as discrete points sampled from a continuum3. The quantum numbers could represent positions along a line, momentum values, or any continuous variable. And the sum is a Riemann sum i.e. the discrete approximation to an integral.

From this perspective, Heisenberg's composition law becomes:

∫ K(x,x'',t₁) K(x'',x',t₂) dx'' = K(x,x',t₁+t₂)

This is the composition law for integral kernels or propagators. The kernel K(x,x',t) describes how amplitude at position x' at time 0 contributes to amplitude at position x at time t. Two successive propagations compose by integrating over all possible intermediate positions x''.

This is the continuous limit of Heisenberg's sum, which is, the language of Green's functions, Feynman propagators, and path integrals, rather than finite-dimensional matrix multiplication. Instead of discrete vectors in Hilbert space, you have continuous amplitude fields ψ(x,t) evolving through spacetime. Instead of abstract operators Ô acting on states, you have integral kernels K(x,x') that can be written down explicitly:

(Ôψ)(x) = ∫ K(x,x') ψ(x') dx'

Originally developed as matrices, what if we had emphasized the kernel interpretation from the start?

3. Why This Didn't Happen

Before proceeding, we must ask an obvious question that if the kernel interpretation was always available, why didn't Heisenberg or his contemporaries pursue it?

The answer is both simple and illuminating they couldn't. There were two (insurmountable) barriers in 1925.

The Philosophical Barrier

Heisenberg was kind of a committed positivist. His entire approach to quantum mechanics was built on rejecting unobservable classical concepts and working only with measurable quantities. In 1925, what you could measure were discrete spectral lines from atoms like specific frequencies and intensities. The quantum numbers n, m, j in his equation labeled these discrete observed states.

They were emphatically not continuous position variables. In fact, Heisenberg actively rejected the notion of continuous position coordinates x. To him, this was classical baggage and precisely the kind of unobservable quantity (like electron orbits) that had led physics astray. His revolutionary step was to eliminate such concepts and build theory entirely on observable transition amplitudes.

4. The crucial missing ingredient was Schrödinger's wavefunction ψ(x) wasn't introduced until January 1926, seven months after Heisenberg's paper. Without the wavefunction, there's no position space to integrate over and no kernel K(x,x') to write down.

The idea of treating his discrete sum as an approximation to a continuous integral would have seemed, to Heisenberg in 1925, like a step backward, like reintroducing the classical continuum he was trying to escape and hence it would have violated the philosophical foundation of his approach.

The Mathematical Barrier

Even if someone had identified and wanted to pursue the kernel interpretation, they literally couldn't have. The essential mathematical ingredient was missing, as I said here, the position-space wavefunction ψ(x) didn't exist yet4.

Erwin Schrödinger wouldn't introduce ψ(x) until January 1926, seven months after Heisenberg's paper, after very rigorous work, which he was the most deeply invested in interpreting it as a literal, physical wave of matter. Without the wavefunction, there's no position space to integrate over, no amplitude field to propagate and no kernel K(x,x') to write down and this kind of kernel interpretation fundamentally requires the continuous coordinate representation that Schrödinger provided.

When Was the Connection Recognized?

By March 1926, Schrödinger proved that his wave mechanics and Heisenberg's matrix mechanics were mathematically equivalent. The connection between matrices and integral operators was quickly established later.

  • Paul Dirac developed transformation theory by 1927, showing how operators could be represented in different bases
  • John von Neumann gave the rigorous mathematical framework by 1932 in his "Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics," explicitly treating operators as kernels and introducing what we now call the rigged Hilbert space

So the relationship between discrete matrices and continuous integral operators was recognized early - by the 1930s at the latest. It appears in Dirac's "Principles of Quantum Mechanics" (1930) and von Neumann's treatise. It's standard material taught in graduate quantum mechanics courses today under names like "coordinate representations," "operators in position basis," or "integral representations." But it wasn't a primary focus until Feynman's Path Integral.

My question is why it never became the primary way of thinking about quantum mechanics (the language taught first, the intuition developed early).

4. The Mathematical Foundations

To understand what's genuinely different between the matrix and kernel formulations, we need to examine the mathematical foundations and this is where I think things get interesting.

The Naive Picture and Its Problems

In elementary treatments, quantum states are said to live in L²(ℝ) - the space of square-integrable functions with ∫|ψ(x)|² dx < ∞. This gives the physically sensible normalization condition for probability densities. Observables like position and momentum are operators acting on these states.

But there's an immediate problem. Consider the momentum operator:

(p̂ψ)(x) = -iℏ dψ/dx

5. Not every square-integrable function is differentiable. You can construct perfectly valid L² functions with discontinuities where dψ/dx doesn't exist. For rigorous QFT, where operator products and renormalization require careful handling of singularities, domain issues are fundamental.

Not every function in L² is differentiable5! You can construct perfectly valid square-integrable functions with discontinuities or kinks where dψ/dx simply doesn't exist. So the momentum operator isn't defined on all of L².

The matrix formulation sweeps this under the rug as you can write p̂|ψ⟩ and move on, trusting that the abstract Hilbert space machinery handles it somehow. The kernel formulation forces you to confront the question immediately on on what domain is this differential operator well-defined?

The Schwartz Space

The rigorous solution begins with the Schwartz space S(ℝ), consisting of infinitely differentiable functions that decay faster than any polynomial:

S = {ψ ∈ C∞ : supx |xn Dk ψ(x)| < ∞ for all n,k ≥ 0}

Examples include Gaussians like ψ(x) = exp(-x²) and essentially all the wavefunctions that appear in solved quantum mechanics problems (harmonic oscillator, hydrogen atom, etc.).

On Schwartz space, everything works perfectly:

  • The momentum operator p̂ = -iℏd/dx maps S → S
  • The position operator x̂ (multiplication by x) maps S → S
  • All compositions are well-defined
  • The Fourier transform maps S → S

But Schwartz space is too restrictive for the full theory. Plane waves exp(ikx) aren't in S (they don't decay). Position eigenstates δ(x-x₀) aren't even functions, they're distributions.

The Rigged Hilbert Space

The complete mathematical structure is the rigged Hilbert space or Gelfand triple:

S ⊂ L² ⊂ S'

where:

  • S (Schwartz space): "nice" test functions where all operators are well-defined
  • L²: normalizable physical states
  • S' (tempered distributions): generalized states including plane waves and delta functions

Operators act in three ways:

  1. Restricted domain: S → S (everything well-defined)
  2. Physical states: L² ⊄→ L² (not everywhere defined, domain issues matter)
  3. Extended domain: S' → S' (generalized eigenfunctions)

This structure of testing functions embedded in square-integrable functions embedded in distributions, is I think what you actually need for rigorous quantum mechanics.

The matrix formulation allows you to ignore this. You can work with abstract vectors |ψ⟩ and trust the formalism. The kernel formulation makes domain questions unavoidable. Is this better or worse?

For standard quantum mechanics, perhaps it doesn't matter. But for quantum field theory, where operator products, renormalization, and UV divergences crucially depend on understanding these singularities, the explicit treatment of domains is essential and the kernel formulation forces you to think about these issues from the start.

5. Comparision

Let me now examine specific problems to see where each formulation excels. They are mathematically equivalent and hence I want to discuss about computational and physical insight.

Matrices:

Quantum Computing / Qubits: A single qubit is a two-dimensional Hilbert space. The state is a 2×1 vector, operators are 2×2 matrices (Pauli matrices), and everything is finite and exact. To formulate this with kernels, you'd need to artificially discretize a continuous space just to get two points, which would be absurd.

Discrete Entanglement: Consider the state (|0,1⟩ + |1,0⟩)/√2 for two qubits. Computing the reduced density matrix via partial trace is trivial in matrix form is just a few lines of algebra. In kernel form, you're explicitly writing out integrals over position space with delta functions.

Symmetry Operations: Group representations act naturally on abstract vectors. Rotation operators, translation operators, Lorentz boosts and hence the mathematics is cleanest in basis-independent Hilbert space formulation.

Kernels:

Scattering Theory: Consider a particle scattering from a delta function potential V(x) = V₀δ(x). In kernel form, you write the Schrödinger equation, match boundary conditions at x=0 (continuity of ψ, jump in dψ/dx), and immediately get the transmission coefficient. In matrix mechanics, you'd need to discretize momentum space, set up a scattering matrix, then take a continuum limit.

6. For scattering problems with continuous spectra, the kernel formulation is the natural setting, and how the calculations are actually done in practice. The matrix picture would require infinite-dimensional matrices with careful limiting procedures.

Wave Packet Evolution: Given an initial Gaussian wavepacket, find ψ(x,t). In kernel form6: ψ(x,t) = ∫K(x,x',t)ψ(x',0)dx' where K is the free particle propagator. Here, you see amplitude flowing through space but in matrix form, you work in momentum basis with abstract states, compute exp(-iĤt/ℏ), then extract position representation.

Path Integrals: Feynman's formulation is explicitly about propagators and the kernel K(x,x',t) computed by summing over all paths. The connection to Heisenberg's composition law is immediate when you think in kernels. In abstract Hilbert space, path integrals feel like a clever mathematical trick rather than the natural extension of the composition principle.

Roughly Equivalent

Harmonic Oscillator: The matrix approach using ladder operators â, â† is elegant for finding all eigenstates efficiently. The kernel approach solving the differential equation -ℏ²/2m ψ'' + mω²x²/2 ψ = Eψ is straightforward for the ground state and works fine for excited states using Hermite polynomials. Which is "better" depends on what you're computing.

Perturbation Theory: Both work fine. Matrix has elegant formulas and kernel has physical visualization of Green's functions.

Conclusion: neither is universally better. They are good at different things and the best quantum mechanics education teaches both perspectives explicitly and teaches to recognize which is more natural for each situation.

6. Example: Uncertainty Principle from Calculus

Let me demonstrate what I mean about different formulations revealing different aspects of physics. Consider the uncertainty principle, one of the most famous results in quantum mechanics.

Standard Textbook Derivation

In most courses, the uncertainty principle ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2 is derived using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in Hilbert space. You define operators x̂ and p̂, compute their commutator [x̂,p̂] = iℏ, then use abstract inner product properties to bound the variances which is elegant but requires the full Hilbert space machinery.

Calculus Derivation

We can derive the same result using nothing but calculus on functions. Let ψ(x) be a smooth, rapidly decaying function (in Schwartz space). Define the spatial spread:

⟨x⟩ = ∫ x|ψ(x)|² dx / ∫ |ψ(x)|² dx

Δx² = ⟨x²⟩ - ⟨x⟩²

And similarly for momentum using the Fourier transform ψ̃(k). The claim is Δx · Δk ≥ 1/2.

7. This derivation uses only integration by parts, Fourier analysis, Cauchy-Schwarz for functions. No Hilbert space or abstract operators and yet we get the same inequality. This reveals uncertainty as a mathematical property of waves that extends beyond the quantum domain.

Proof7: Without loss of generality, assume ⟨x⟩ = 0 and ⟨k⟩ = 0 (shift coordinates), and normalize ∫|ψ|² = 1. Consider the integral, integrating by parts (using compact support/rapid decay):

2 Im[∫ x · ψ* · ψ' dx] = ∫ |ψ|² dx = 1

Therefore: |∫ x · ψ* · ψ' dx| ≥ 1/2

Now apply Cauchy-Schwarz (the calculus version for functions):

|∫ u(x)v(x) dx|² ≤ (∫|u|² dx)(∫|v|² dx)

With u = xψ and v = ψ':

1/4 ≤ (∫ x²|ψ|² dx)(∫ |ψ'|² dx) = Δx² · Δk²

Therefore Δx · Δk ≥ 1/2, which gives Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2 with proper units.

What's Different About This Derivation?

The mathematical content is equivalent because we used Cauchy-Schwarz either way. But the physical interpretation shifts in the sense of:

Hilbert space version: Uncertainty is about non-commuting operators. It's tied to the quantum formalism, to measurement, to the act of observation.

Calculus version: Uncertainty is a mathematical fact about smooth functions and their Fourier transforms. Any function ψ(x) and its Fourier transform ψ̃(k) cannot both be arbitrarily concentrated. This is wave mechanics, a property of waves that extends beyond quantum mechanics.

The amplitude field ψ(x) has spatial extent Δx. Its momentum-space representation has extent Δp. These spreads are inversely related by the mathematics of waves. This would be true even if quantum mechanics didn't exist and it's a property of the Fourier transform.

When an observer with incomplete information about the field tries to make predictions, they encounter this spread as "uncertainty." But in the kernel formulation, the uncertainty is in the observer's knowledge, located in the observer's knowledge, while the field itself evolves deterministically according to the Schrödinger equation.

This shift in perspective that uncertainty as mathematical spread of fields rather than intrinsic observer effect, might seem subtle, but it shapes how you think about quantum mechanics, especially when considering interpretations and foundations.

7. Path Integrals

Perhaps the most striking consequence of emphasizing the kernel formulation is that Feynman's path integral was implicit in Heisenberg's composition law all along.

Return to the kernel composition law:

∫ K(x,x'',t₁) K(x'',x',t₂) dx'' = K(x,x',t₁+t₂)

To propagate from position x' at time 0 to position x at time t₁+t₂, you integrate over all possible intermediate positions x'' at intermediate time t₁. The amplitude to go from x' to x is the sum of amplitudes for going x' → x'' → x over all possible intermediate points.

8. Feynman's path integral was implicit in Heisenberg's 1925 composition law from the very beginning. If kernels had been emphasized from the start, path integrals would have been the obvious next step i.e. "just keep slicing time finer until you have a continuum of paths."

Now apply this composition rule repeatedly8. Divide the time interval [0,t] into N small slices of duration ε = t/N:

K(x,x',t) = ∫...∫ K(x,xN-1,ε) K(xN-1,xN-2,ε) ... K(x1,x',ε) dx1...dxN-1

You sum over all possible sequences of positions (x', x₁, x₂, ..., xN-1, x) - every discrete path from x' to x.

For a free particle, the short-time propagator has the form:

K(x,x',ε) ≈ √(m/2πiℏε) exp(im(x-x')²/2ℏε)

The exponent is precisely (action/ℏ) for that time slice: S = ½m(Δx/Δt)²·Δt.

In the continuum limit N → ∞, ε → 0, the sum over discrete paths becomes an integral over all continuous paths:

K(x,x',t) = ∫ exp(iS[path]/ℏ) 𝒟[path]

where S is the classical action ∫L dt integrated along the path.

This is Feynman's path integral formulation of quantum mechanics, which in retrospect, was already implicit in Heisenberg's 1925 composition law, waiting to be recognized.

If the kernel picture had been emphasized from 1926 onward, path integrals would have been the obvious next step: "Just keep slicing time finer until you have a continuum of paths summed over." Instead, when Feynman introduced path integrals, many physicists found them exotic and mysterious and as a strange reformulation rather than the natural extension of the composition principle.

The mathematical structure was always there as Heisenberg's sum over intermediate states IS a path integral in discrete form. The continuous version just makes this manifest but you only see it clearly if you're thinking in terms of kernels from the start.

8. Probability and Measurement: Information-Theoretic View

One of the most philosophically charged aspects of quantum mechanics is probability. In the standard formulation, probability is postulated via the Born rule: P = |⟨eigenstate|ψ⟩|². This is an axiom of QM.

But in the kernel formulation, something interesting emerges. If we take seriously the idea that ψ(x,t) is a deterministic field evolving via the Schrödinger equation everywhere in spacetime, then probability is epistemic, arising from observer limitations rather than baked into the physics.

My Argument

The amplitude field ψ(x,t) evolves deterministically: iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ. Which is a differential equation. Given initial conditions ψ(x,0), the field is determined everywhere in spacetime and there's no randomness in the dynamics because it's just smooth, continuous evolution.

When a "measurement" occurs, say, coupling to an apparatus, what actually happens is entanglement. The system and apparatus states become correlated:

ψtotal(xsystem, xapparatus, t) = Σi ci φi(xsystem)χi(xapparatus)

9. This argument remains "controversial" because it requires accepting many-worlds interpretation and certain decision-theoretic principles. But, I think it's conceptually cleaner than simply asserting the Born rule as axiom. The work of Deutsch, Wallace, and Zurek on deriving Born rule from quantum mechanics plus decision theory is active research and not settled fact.

All the terms exist and the field has "branched" into multiple peaks in the joint configuration space (xsystem, xapparatus). An observer, who is part of the apparatus state, finds themselves in ONE of these branches, say the k-th one9.

But which one? The observer doesn't know. From their perspective inside a branch, they have incomplete information. They know:

  • The field evolved deterministically according to Schrödinger's equation
  • The field has multiple branches with amplitudes {ci}
  • They are experiencing branch k

But they don't know which k they ended up in until they look at the apparatus.

To make predictions about future measurements, the observer needs a probability distribution over "which branch am I on?" Given only the amplitudes ci and requiring that no branch is privileged a priori (symmetry), information theory suggests the probability should be proportional to |ci|².

This is the Born rule, but derived from self-location uncertainty in a deterministic branching field, not postulated as a fundamental axiom.

Critical Caveats

I must be careful here because this derivation remains controversial. It requires assumptions of decision-theoretic rationality (observers act to maximize expected utility), certain symmetry principles (branch-counting measures), and acceptance of the Everett/many-worlds interpretation.

Not everyone accepts these premises and some find the arguments circular or question-begging. But I think this approach is conceptually cleaner than simply asserting the Born rule as an axiom. And it's more natural to state in the kernel formulation where:

  1. The field ψ(x,t) is the fundamental object (not abstract state vectors)
  2. Evolution is always continuous and deterministic (not punctuated by collapse)
  3. Probability emerges from observer perspective (not baked into the physics)

Does This Solve the Measurement Problem?

No. We still need to explain why observers experience discreteness when the field is continuous and contains all branches. Why does it feel like there's a collapse when there isn't one in the dynamics?

But the kernel formulation shifts the problem from "why does evolution become probabilistic?" to "why does deterministic field branching feel probabilistic to embedded observers?" That is a different kind of question, one about consciousness and experience rather than about physics per se. Whether this is progress or just moving the mystery is debatable.

What's not debatable is that the formulation affects which aspects of the problem seem salient and which solutions seem natural. The kernel picture makes many-worlds interpretations feel more natural because you're already thinking of ψ as a physical field that always evolves smoothly.

9. Consequences

If matrices and kernels are equivalent, and experts already use both, what's the practical value of this analysis?

Quantum Field Theory and Renormalization

In QFT, you're already working with fields φ(x,t), and the natural objects are propagators like Green's functions, Feynman diagrams are built from propagators and Perturbation theory expands scattering amplitudes as sums of diagrams and hence you could say the entire structure is kernel-based.

If students learned quantum mechanics with kernels emphasized from the start, the transition to QFT would be smoother. "Oh, we're just going to field configurations with variable particle number" instead of the mysterious "second quantization" that textbooks introduce. The path from QM to QFT would feel continuous rather than requiring a conceptual leap.

Wheeler-DeWitt Equation and Quantum Gravity

I think this is the most important. General relativity says spacetime itself is dynamical and there's no external time parameter and time is part of what you're trying to quantize. In standard quantum mechanics with explicit time evolution |ψ(t)⟩ = exp(-iĤt/ℏ)|ψ(0)⟩, trying to quantize gravity leads to the Wheeler-DeWitt equation: Ĥψ[gμν] = 0. A constraint equation with no time, no evolution and no clear probability interpretation which is rather a crisis in the standard formulation.

In the kernel picture, you already think of ψ(x,t) as a field on spacetime, and not a state evolving in time. The generalization to ψ[gμν] as a functional on spacetime geometries feels more natural and you're treating space and time democratically from the beginning, and not trying to retrofit time into the dynamics.

Would this solve the problem of time in quantum gravity? Obviously not. But it might make the conceptual problems less jarring, which matters when you're groping in the dark for the right formulation.

Analog Quantum Simulators

Modern quantum simulation experiments with cold atoms, trapped ions, and superconducting circuits directly measure correlation functions: G(x,x',t,t') = ⟨ψ†(x,t)ψ(x',t')⟩ and these are Kernels. Experimentalists are measuring propagators and two-point functions rather than state vectors.

The standard theoretical analysis pipeline is: Measure correlation functions and then reconstruct the full many-body wavefunction (exponentially hard) and extract physics from the wavefunction

But the correlations ARE the physics. A kernel-first approach would: Measure kernels/propagators directly and extract physical properties (transport coefficients, localization lengths, thermalization rates) from kernel structure and then skip the exponential state reconstruction

This is "kernel tomography" i.e. characterizing quantum systems via their Green's functions rather than wavefunctions. For many-body systems in condensed matter, this could be computationally more efficient and experimentally more direct.

Pedagogical Value:

Even if this doesn't lead to new physics, it could improve how we learn quantum mechanics. Graduate students currently have to discover the equivalence on their own, often years into their training.

10. Verdict

Let me close with what this exploration amounts to.

Limitations

The relationship between matrices and integral kernels was settled by Dirac and von Neumann by the early 1930s, so nothing here is new ground. The kernel formulation shifts how the measurement problem feels, but leaves it unsolved as the Born rule still needs to be introduced, either as axiom or via controversial derivations. Matrices excel for discrete systems; kernels excel for continuous ones, and anyone working in QFT, scattering theory, or Green's function methods already uses kernels extensively.

11. Conclusion

But the alternative path: interpreting the sum as integral kernel composition, was always there. It wasn't pursued initially because the wavefunction didn't exist yet. By the time Schrödinger introduced ψ(x), the matrix formulation was already established, and the kernel picture became a secondary representation rather than the primary one.

This matters because formalism shapes physics. The tools you learn first determine which problems you think to attack, which solutions seem obvious, which directions seem promising. Had the kernel formulation been emphasized equally from the start, maybe path integrals might have arrived sooner, QFT might have seemed less mysterious from the outset, and the tension with quantum gravity, which stems partly from assuming an external time, might have surfaced earlier. Quantum computing, on the other hand, might have developed more slowly without abstract Hilbert space as the default language.
